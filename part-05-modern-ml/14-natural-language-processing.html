14  Natural Language Processing: From Words to Meaningful Representations

14.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

  • explain why human language creates unusual machine-learning challenges;
  • define corpus, document, token, vocabulary, n-gram, and document-term matrix;
  • distinguish tokenization from normalization;
  • explain the tradeoffs of stop-word removal, stemming, and lemmatization;
  • construct bag-of-words and TF-IDF representations;
  • derive and interpret term frequency and inverse document frequency;
  • explain sparsity and high dimensionality in text data;
  • use cosine similarity to compare document vectors;
  • build leakage-safe text-classification pipelines;
  • evaluate text classifiers with metrics appropriate to the task;
  • inspect model coefficients to understand lexical signals;
  • distinguish lexical association from semantic understanding;
  • explain distributed word embeddings conceptually;
  • connect embeddings to representation learning from Chapter 13;
  • explain contextual embeddings and why a word can require different representations in different sentences;
  • derive the intuition behind self-attention;
  • explain queries, keys, values, and attention weights;
  • describe the role of positional information in transformer models;
  • distinguish encoder-style, decoder-style, and sequence-to-sequence transformer objectives at a high level;
  • recognize data leakage, spurious lexical cues, subgroup harms, and construct-validity problems in NLP;
  • design reproducible NLP studies using health, education, or public benchmark text.
  • distinguish language-only NLP from multimodal learning that jointly represents text and visual information.

14.2 Why Language Is Different

Most earlier chapters assumed that observations were already represented as columns of numbers.

Language does not arrive that way.

A student writes:

“I understand the material, but I still feel lost when I try to apply it.”

A patient writes:

“The medication helped the pain, but I have been exhausted since Tuesday.”

A scientific abstract contains arguments, terminology, uncertainty, relationships, and context.

Before a machine-learning algorithm can operate on these texts, we must construct a numerical representation.

The NLP pipeline therefore begins with a profound design problem:

\[ \text{language} \longrightarrow \text{representation} \longrightarrow \text{model}. \]

ImportantCentral Question

When language becomes numbers, which aspects of meaning survive the transformation—and which disappear?

14.3 The Unit of Analysis

Before tokenizing anything, identify the analytical unit.

Possible units include:

  • an entire document;
  • a paragraph;
  • a sentence;
  • an utterance;
  • a social-media post;
  • a clinical note;
  • a discussion-board contribution;
  • a token.

The unit must align with the research question.

Predicting a document-level label from sentence fragments changes the problem.

14.4 Core Vocabulary

A corpus is a collection of text documents.

A document is one analytical text unit.

A token is a unit produced by tokenization.

A vocabulary is the set of distinct tokens/features represented by the model.

An n-gram is a contiguous sequence of \(n\) tokens.

Examples:

  • unigram: machine
  • bigram: machine learning
  • trigram: machine learning model

14.5 Tokenization

Tokenization divides text into units.

A simple whitespace tokenizer might turn:

Machine learning changes research.

into:

Machine
learning
changes
research.

But real tokenization must confront:

  • punctuation;
  • contractions;
  • hyphenation;
  • URLs;
  • emojis;
  • hashtags;
  • numbers;
  • abbreviations;
  • multilingual text;
  • domain terminology.

Modern subword tokenizers go further by representing words through reusable pieces.

Tokenization is therefore not clerical preprocessing. It defines the symbols available to the model.

14.6 A Simple Tokenization Demonstration

import re

text = """
Machine learning can support research,
but representation choices matter.
"""

tokens = re.findall(
    r"\b[\w'-]+\b",
    text.lower()
)

tokens
['machine',
 'learning',
 'can',
 'support',
 'research',
 'but',
 'representation',
 'choices',
 'matter']

This tokenizer is intentionally simple. Production NLP systems often require more sophisticated rules or learned subword tokenizers.

14.7 Text Normalization

Common transformations include:

  • lowercasing;
  • punctuation handling;
  • number normalization;
  • spelling normalization;
  • stop-word removal;
  • stemming;
  • lemmatization.

Each transformation changes the information available to the model.

For example, removing the word not could reverse sentiment.

Aggressive normalization can improve statistical efficiency while destroying useful meaning.

14.8 Stop Words

Stop words are frequently occurring function words such as the, and, or is.

Removing them can reduce dimensionality in some lexical models.

But consider:

“The treatment was effective.”

versus

“The treatment was not effective.”

A generic stop-word list that removes negation could be disastrous.

Domain context matters.

14.9 Stemming and Lemmatization

Stemming applies heuristic rules to reduce words to stems.

Lemmatization attempts to map inflected words to linguistically meaningful base forms.

For example:

\[ \{\text{learn},\text{learned},\text{learning}\} \rightarrow \text{learn} \]

may be useful in some applications.

But modern contextual models often operate without traditional stemming or lemmatization.

Preprocessing should follow the representation and task.

14.10 Bag-of-Words Foundations

14.11 From Documents to Counts

Suppose our corpus contains:

  1. machine learning supports research
  2. research supports learning
  3. machine learning requires data

A bag-of-words representation records token counts while largely ignoring order.

If the vocabulary is

\[ V= \{ \text{machine}, \text{learning}, \text{supports}, \text{research}, \text{requires}, \text{data} \}, \]

each document becomes a numerical vector.

14.12 CountVectorizer

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
import pandas as pd

documents = [
    "machine learning supports research",
    "research supports learning",
    "machine learning requires data"
]

count_vectorizer = CountVectorizer()
X_count = count_vectorizer.fit_transform(
    documents
)

count_df = pd.DataFrame(
    X_count.toarray(),
    columns=count_vectorizer.get_feature_names_out()
)

count_df
data learning machine requires research supports
0 0 1 1 0 1 1
1 0 1 0 0 1 1
2 1 1 1 1 0 0

This is a document-term matrix.

Rows represent documents.

Columns represent vocabulary features.

14.13 Sparsity

Real vocabularies can contain tens or hundreds of thousands of features.

Any individual document uses only a small fraction of them.

The resulting matrix is sparse.

If

\[ X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}, \]

\(p\) may be enormous while most entries equal zero.

Sparse matrix representations avoid storing every zero explicitly.

This is one reason we should generally avoid converting large text matrices with .toarray().

We did so above only because the teaching example is tiny.

14.14 What Bag of Words Forgets

Compare:

student understands model

and

model understands student

A unigram bag-of-words representation can treat them identically.

Thus:

\[ \boxed{\text{same token counts}} \not\Rightarrow \boxed{\text{same meaning}}. \]

14.15 N-Grams Restore Some Local Order

bigram_vectorizer = CountVectorizer(
    ngram_range=(1, 2)
)

X_bigram = bigram_vectorizer.fit_transform(
    documents
)

bigram_vectorizer.get_feature_names_out()
array(['data', 'learning', 'learning requires', 'learning supports',
       'machine', 'machine learning', 'requires', 'requires data',
       'research', 'research supports', 'supports', 'supports learning',
       'supports research'], dtype=object)

Bigrams can preserve phrases such as machine learning.

But vocabulary size grows rapidly.

This creates a tradeoff between richer local context and dimensionality/sparsity.

14.16 TF-IDF

14.17 Why Counts Are Not Enough

A term appearing frequently in every document may be less discriminating than a term concentrated in a smaller subset.

TF-IDF combines term-specific evidence with corpus-level rarity, reflecting a long information-retrieval tradition of term weighting (Salton and Buckley 1988):

  • term frequency — importance within a document;
  • inverse document frequency — rarity across documents.

14.18 Term Frequency

A simple term frequency is

\[ TF(t,d) = \text{count of term }t\text{ in document }d. \]

Variants normalize by document length or use sublinear scaling.

14.19 Inverse Document Frequency

A common smoothed form is

\[ IDF(t) = \log \left( \frac{1+n} {1+df(t)} \right) +1, \]

where:

  • \(n\) is the number of documents;
  • \(df(t)\) is the number of documents containing term \(t\).

Terms appearing in fewer documents receive greater IDF weight.

14.20 TF-IDF Weight

\[ TFIDF(t,d) = TF(t,d)\times IDF(t). \]

The exact implementation can vary, so reproducible research should document the software and parameterization.

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer

14.22 Cosine Similarity

Text vectors are often compared using cosine similarity.

For vectors \(x\) and \(y\),

\[ \cos(\theta) = \frac{x^\top y} {\|x\|_2\|y\|_2}. \]

Cosine similarity focuses on orientation rather than raw vector magnitude.

14.23 Document Similarity

from sklearn.metrics.pairwise import cosine_similarity

    X_tfidf
)

pd.DataFrame(

    index=["Doc 1", "Doc 2", "Doc 3"],

A high cosine similarity means the documents have similar vector directions under the chosen representation.

It does not prove that humans would judge the documents semantically equivalent.

14.27 Leakage-Safe NLP Pipeline

The vectorizer must learn vocabulary and IDF weights from the training data only.

from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(
    texts,
    labels,
    test_size=.30,
    random_state=42,
    stratify=labels
)

text_model = Pipeline([
    (
        "tfidf",
        TfidfVectorizer(
            ngram_range=(1, 2),
            min_df=1
        )
    ),
    (
        "model",
        LogisticRegression(
            max_iter=2000
        )
    )
])

text_model.fit(
    X_train,
    y_train
)

text_prob = text_model.predict_proba(
    X_test
)[:, 1]

text_pred = text_model.predict(
    X_test
)

This is the text equivalent of the leakage-safe pipelines developed earlier.

14.30 Spurious Lexical Cues

Suppose all essays receiving low scores happened to contain a particular course code because one instructor used a different prompt.

A classifier might learn the course code rather than writing quality.

Text models are especially vulnerable to shortcuts involving:

  • source;
  • author;
  • template;
  • timestamp;
  • institution;
  • formatting;
  • demographic proxies;
  • label artifacts.

High predictive accuracy can coexist with invalid measurement.

14.31 Distributed Representations

14.32 From Sparse Counts to Dense Vectors

Bag-of-words and TF-IDF create sparse vectors whose dimensions correspond to vocabulary features.

Distributed embeddings instead represent tokens using dense vectors:

\[ w \longrightarrow e_w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}, \]

where typically

\[ d\ll |V|. \]

Words appearing in similar linguistic contexts can acquire nearby representations.

14.33 The Distributional Idea

A foundational intuition is:

words used in similar contexts often have related meanings.

Rather than manually defining semantic categories, representation learning can infer statistical regularities from large corpora.

This changes the representation from:

\[ \text{word identity} \]

toward:

\[ \text{position in a learned semantic space}. \]

14.34 One-Hot Versus Dense Representation

For a vocabulary of size \(V\), a one-hot vector contains one 1 and \(V-1\) zeros.

For example:

\[ \text{data} \rightarrow [0,0,1,0,\ldots,0]. \]

The one-hot representation does not encode similarity directly.

An embedding might instead be:

\[ \text{data} \rightarrow [0.18,-0.42,0.73,\ldots]. \]

The coordinates are learned rather than manually assigned.

14.35 Word2Vec Intuition

Word2Vec helped popularize efficient learning of distributed word representations from large corpora (Mikolov et al. 2013). Two classic Word2Vec objectives are:

  • Continuous Bag of Words (CBOW): predict a target word from surrounding context;
  • Skip-Gram: predict surrounding context from a target word.

The training task is instrumental.

The learned embedding matrix becomes the useful artifact.

This is an important pattern in modern machine learning:

A model can learn a representation through a prediction task different from the downstream task we ultimately care about.

14.36 Static Embeddings Have a Limitation

Consider:

“She deposited money at the bank.”

and

“They sat on the bank of the river.”

A static embedding assigns one vector to bank.

But meaning depends on context.

We therefore want:

\[ e(\text{bank}\mid\text{sentence 1}) \neq e(\text{bank}\mid\text{sentence 2}). \]

This motivates contextual embeddings.

14.37 Attention

14.38 Why Context Requires Interaction

To represent a word contextually, the model needs a mechanism through which tokens can influence one another.

Attention provides such a mechanism.

For each token representation, a model constructs:

  • a query;
  • a key;
  • a value.

For matrix \(X\):

\[ Q=XW_Q, \]

\[ K=XW_K, \]

\[ V=XW_V. \]

14.39 Scaled Dot-Product Attention

Attention is commonly written as

\[ Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax \left( \frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right)V. \]

This compact equation is central to modern NLP.

Let’s unpack it.

14.40 Step 1: Compare Queries and Keys

The matrix

\[ QK^\top \]

contains compatibility scores between token queries and token keys.

A large dot product suggests a stronger relationship under the learned projections.

14.41 Step 2: Scale

Divide by

\[ \sqrt{d_k}. \]

Without scaling, dot products can become large as dimensionality increases, pushing softmax toward extreme values and making optimization less stable.

14.42 Step 3: Softmax

For each query, softmax converts scores into weights:

\[ \alpha_{ij} = \frac{ \exp(s_{ij}) }{ \sum_m\exp(s_{im}) }. \]

The weights sum to one across the attended positions.

14.43 Step 4: Weighted Values

The contextual output for token \(i\) is

\[ h_i = \sum_j \alpha_{ij}v_j. \]

A token’s representation is therefore updated using information from other tokens.

14.44 Attention by Hand

tokens = [
    "student",
    "understood",
    "the",
    "model"
]

scores = np.array([
    [2.2, 1.0, 0.2, 0.8],
    [0.7, 2.1, 0.1, 1.8],
    [0.4, 0.5, 1.4, 0.6],
    [0.9, 1.7, 0.2, 2.3]
])

def row_softmax(matrix):
    shifted = matrix - matrix.max(
        axis=1,
        keepdims=True
    )
    exp_values = np.exp(shifted)
    return exp_values / exp_values.sum(
        axis=1,
        keepdims=True
    )

attention_weights = row_softmax(
    scores
)

plt.figure(figsize=(7, 5))
plt.imshow(
    attention_weights,
    aspect="auto"
)
plt.xticks(
    range(len(tokens)),
    tokens,
    rotation=30
)
plt.yticks(
    range(len(tokens)),
    tokens
)
plt.xlabel("Key token")
plt.ylabel("Query token")
plt.title("Toy Attention Weights")
plt.colorbar(label="Attention weight")
plt.show()

These weights are constructed for teaching rather than learned from a trained language model.

14.45 Attention Is Not Automatically Explanation

An attention weight tells us something about a model’s internal computation.

It should not automatically be interpreted as:

  • human reasoning;
  • causal importance;
  • psychological attention;
  • a complete explanation of a prediction.

Model internals and scientific explanations are different objects.

14.46 Transformers

14.47 The Transformer Idea

The transformer architecture builds representations using attention rather than relying exclusively on recurrence (Vaswani et al. 2017).

A simplified transformer block includes:

\[ \text{token representations} \rightarrow \text{multi-head attention} \rightarrow \text{feed-forward network} \rightarrow \text{updated representations}. \]

Residual connections and normalization support optimization.

14.48 Multi-Head Attention

Instead of computing one attention pattern, transformers can compute multiple heads:

\[ head_i = Attention( QW_i^Q, KW_i^K, VW_i^V ). \]

The heads are combined:

\[ MultiHead(Q,K,V) = Concat(head_1,\ldots,head_h)W^O. \]

Different heads can learn different interaction patterns.

We should be cautious about assigning simple linguistic meanings to individual heads without evidence.

14.49 Positional Information

Self-attention alone does not inherently encode token order in the same way recurrence does.

Transformers therefore incorporate positional information.

Conceptually:

\[ \text{input representation} = \text{token embedding} + \text{position representation}. \]

Modern architectures implement positional information in several ways.

14.50 Encoder-Style Models

Encoder-style transformers are especially suited to constructing contextual representations from an input sequence.

Typical applications include:

  • classification;
  • token labeling;
  • semantic similarity;
  • information extraction.

BERT is a historically important example of this family (Devlin et al. 2019).

14.51 Decoder-Style Models

Decoder-style transformers predict tokens autoregressively:

\[ P(x_1,\ldots,x_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} P( x_t \mid x_1,\ldots,x_{t-1} ). \]

This next-token-prediction structure forms a foundation for many modern generative language models.

We develop that much more deeply in Chapter 15.

14.52 Encoder-Decoder Models

Sequence-to-sequence transformers can use an encoder to represent an input and a decoder to generate an output.

Applications include:

  • translation;
  • summarization;
  • structured generation.

14.53 From NLP to Foundation Models

The progression across this chapter can now be seen as a history of increasingly expressive representations:

\[ \text{counts} \rightarrow \text{TF-IDF} \rightarrow \text{static embeddings} \rightarrow \text{contextual embeddings} \rightarrow \text{transformers}. \]

Each step preserves different information and introduces new assumptions, computational costs, and opportunities.

14.54 Research-Grade NLP

14.55 Split by the Real Generalization Boundary

Randomly splitting documents is not always appropriate.

Suppose several notes come from the same patient, essays from the same student, or posts from the same author.

If documents from one person occur in both train and test sets, performance may partly reflect recognition of the person rather than generalization to new people.

Possible split boundaries include:

  • person;
  • institution;
  • course;
  • instructor;
  • time;
  • geographic site;
  • publication venue.

The split should emulate the deployment or scientific generalization claim.

14.56 Duplicate and Near-Duplicate Text

Copied templates, boilerplate, repeated prompts, quoted material, and duplicated documents can inflate performance.

Text research should inspect overlap before modeling.

14.57 Temporal Leakage

Language changes over time.

A model may exploit information unavailable at the intended prediction time.

Examples include:

  • later clinical diagnoses appearing in notes;
  • instructor feedback written after grading;
  • references to final outcomes;
  • retrospective summaries.

Define the prediction timestamp before selecting text.

14.58 Construct Validity

Suppose a researcher predicts “student engagement” from discussion posts.

What exactly is the label?

Attendance?

Instructor rating?

Post frequency?

Self-report?

Course completion?

The sophistication of the language model cannot repair a weak construct definition.

14.59 Fairness and Language Variation

Language reflects:

  • culture;
  • dialect;
  • educational experience;
  • profession;
  • age;
  • geography;
  • disability-related communication patterns;
  • multilingual backgrounds.

A text model may interpret legitimate language variation as predictive signal.

Responsible evaluation should examine relevant subgroups and avoid treating one linguistic norm as inherently superior.

14.60 From NLP to Multimodal Learning

Natural-language processing focuses on language, while computer vision focuses on visual information. Many modern systems operate across both.

A multimodal system may learn from paired observations such as image-text pairs; contrastive vision-language systems such as CLIP demonstrate how aligned representations can be learned from large collections of such pairs (Radford et al. 2021).

For example, it may learn from

\[ (\text{image},\text{text}), \]

or combine several modalities:

\[ x_i= \left( x_i^{\text{text}}, x_i^{\text{image}}, x_i^{\text{audio}} \right). \]

Applications include image captioning, text-to-image retrieval, visual question answering, medical image–text analysis, and interpreting diagrams together with written explanations.

14.60.1 Shared Spaces and Cross-Modal Alignment

One strategy is to learn representations in which related text and images are close in a shared embedding space. Conceptually,

\[ f_{\text{text}}(x_i^{\text{text}}) \approx f_{\text{image}}(x_i^{\text{image}}) \]

when the observations describe related content.

This bridges dimensionality reduction and representation learning with the language representations developed in this chapter.

ImportantResearch Reality Check

A multimodal model that aligns an image with a caption has learned useful statistical correspondence. That does not establish that the system interprets either modality as a human does, nor that its representations are free from biases inherited from image-text data.

14.60.2 Learning Experience: Design a Multimodal Study

Choose one book track and specify the unit of analysis, text modality, visual modality, analytical objective, representation of each modality, alignment strategy, one leakage risk, one construct-validity concern, one domain-shift concern, and the evidence required before making a substantive claim.

Useful public benchmarks include 20 Newsgroups and other openly licensed text-classification corpora.

14.62 Public Research Dataset Studio

For publishable research, students should not merely download a famous dataset and chase accuracy.

A stronger workflow is:

  1. identify a substantive research question;
  2. establish the unit of analysis;
  3. document dataset provenance and license;
  4. define the construct and target;
  5. specify the real generalization boundary;
  6. create a transparent baseline;
  7. compare representations;
  8. conduct error analysis;
  9. examine robustness;
  10. evaluate subgroup performance where justified;
  11. quantify uncertainty;
  12. document computational resources;
  13. make code and derived artifacts reproducible where permitted.

This creates a path from classroom NLP to conference-quality research.

14.63 Beyond the Algorithm: Does a Vector Understand a Word?

NoteRepresentation and Meaning

Suppose a model places two words close together in a 768-dimensional space.

What has been demonstrated?

At minimum, the model has learned a statistical representation in which the words behave similarly according to its training objective and data.

That can be extraordinarily useful.

But usefulness does not settle the philosophical question of understanding.

Human language is connected to:

  • embodied experience;
  • intention;
  • culture;
  • social relationships;
  • history;
  • goals;
  • consequences.

A machine representation can encode remarkable linguistic regularities without giving us a simple answer to whether the system “understands” in the human sense.

For data science, the practical lesson is not to resolve centuries of philosophy of language.

It is to maintain conceptual discipline:

\[ \boxed{ \text{successful representation} \neq \text{proof of human-like understanding} } \]

A model should be credited for what evidence demonstrates—not for metaphysical properties we inferred from impressive output.

14.64 Text Is Not the Same as Meaning

Natural-language processing transforms language into representations algorithms can process:

\[ \text{Language}\rightarrow\text{Representation}\rightarrow\text{Model}\rightarrow\text{Prediction or Generation}. \]

Every representation preserves some distinctions and transforms or discards others. Bag-of-words emphasizes lexical occurrence; embeddings encode learned relationships; contextual representations allow the same token to differ across contexts.

\[ \boxed{\text{Representation of Language}\neq\text{Language Itself}} \]

ImportantResearch Reality Check

High accuracy on a text benchmark does not establish that a model understands the underlying human construct. Test whether performance depends on intended linguistic evidence or shortcuts such as formatting, source, demographic proxies, repeated phrases, or dataset artifacts.

14.65 AI Pair Programmer: The Model Understands Students

An AI assistant analyzes open-ended student comments and produces a classifier with high accuracy.

It concludes:

“The model understands how students feel about the course.”

Audit the statement.

Ask:

  • What was the target?
  • How was sentiment or experience operationalized?
  • Who labeled the data?
  • Could the model exploit lexical shortcuts?
  • Were multiple comments from the same student separated across train and test?
  • Are dialect and multilingual variation represented?
  • Was performance evaluated by subgroup?
  • Were probabilities calibrated?
  • What errors matter most?
  • Does predictive success establish understanding?

Rewrite the conclusion into defensible scientific language.

14.73 Key Takeaways

  • NLP begins by converting language into numerical representations.
  • Tokenization and normalization determine what information becomes available to a model.
  • Bag-of-words models are simple, sparse, and surprisingly strong baselines.
  • TF-IDF balances within-document frequency against corpus-wide prevalence.
  • Cosine similarity compares vector orientation rather than raw magnitude.
  • Text vectorization belongs inside leakage-safe pipelines.
  • High accuracy can arise from spurious lexical shortcuts.
  • Dense embeddings learn distributed representations from statistical context.
  • Contextual embeddings allow the representation of a token to depend on surrounding language.
  • Self-attention uses learned query-key relationships to combine value representations.
  • Transformers build contextual representations through attention and feed-forward transformations.
  • Attention weights are not automatically explanations.
  • NLP studies require careful unit-of-analysis, split-boundary, construct-validity, and duplicate-contamination decisions.
  • Language variation creates important fairness considerations.
  • Successful linguistic representation does not by itself establish human-like understanding.
  • NLP performance depends on representational and data-design choices.
  • Strong benchmark performance does not establish human-like understanding or construct validity.
